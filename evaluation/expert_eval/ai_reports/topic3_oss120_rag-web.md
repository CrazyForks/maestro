# Artificial Intelligence in Hospital Management: Scheduling, Resource Allocation, Patient Flow, and Evidence Gaps

# 1. Introduction & Scope

Artificial intelligence is increasingly applied to hospital management by providing data‑driven decision support for operational processes that have traditionally relied on manual planning and static protocols. Recent studies have examined AI techniques—including machine‑learning classifiers, reinforcement‑learning policies, and optimisation heuristics—that aim to improve efficiency, safety, and cost‑effectiveness of clinical and non‑clinical workflows, thereby addressing challenges such as resource scarcity, variability in patient demand, and prolonged wait times [30][90].

Three interrelated operational pillars constitute the core of AI‑enabled hospital management. The first pillar, scheduling, includes appointment, operating‑room, staff‑shift, and equipment‑booking coordination, where predictive analytics and real‑time rescheduling mechanisms are used to align capacity with fluctuating demand. The second pillar, resource allocation and capacity management, involves bed and intensive‑care unit planning, dynamic staffing, inventory optimisation, surge‑capacity planning, and predictive maintenance of critical assets, all of which depend on demand forecasts and optimisation engines. The third pillar, patient‑flow optimisation, integrates risk‑stratification, intra‑hospital logistics, automated documentation, and closed‑loop feedback systems to streamline the admission‑to‑discharge continuum. These pillars are interdependent; improvements in one domain can affect the others, influencing overall hospital performance [30][75].

Emerging AI technologies provide additional capabilities for the three pillars. Generative‑AI models, such as large‑language and diffusion models, support natural‑language interfaces, automated protocol generation, and scenario simulation that can augment scheduling and workflow design. Federated learning offers a privacy‑preserving approach for training shared models across multiple hospitals without exchanging raw patient data, which can support cross‑institutional improvements in demand forecasting and resource‑allocation policies. Real‑time digital twins create continuously synchronised virtual replicas of hospital operations, combining physics‑informed simulation with live data streams to deliver descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics for capacity planning and optimisation [31][83].

This review addresses the following research questions: (1) How do AI methods support each of the three operational pillars, and what evidence exists regarding their impact on key performance indicators such as wait‑time reduction, bed‑turnover, and cost savings? (2) How can generative AI, federated learning, and digital‑twin technologies be integrated to enhance the effectiveness and scalability of AI‑enabled hospital management? (3) What gaps remain in the empirical literature—particularly concerning multi‑site, longitudinal evidence, economic evaluation, and governance—that must be addressed to develop an integrated, evidence‑based roadmap for future implementation?

Addressing these questions is necessary for constructing a phased implementation strategy that aligns technical feasibility with regulatory, ethical, and organisational considerations. By synthesising evidence across scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow optimisation, and by mapping emerging technologies onto these domains, the review aims to provide researchers and practitioners with a reference for designing, evaluating, and scaling AI‑driven solutions in hospital settings.

Having established the scope and objectives of this review, the next section details the methodological approach employed to identify, select, and analyse the relevant literature.

# 2. Methodology

The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework and follows the PRISMA‑S extension together with the PRISMA‑2020 checklist to ensure transparent, reproducible reporting of all methodological steps [30][90].

A comprehensive set of information sources was searched to capture both clinical and engineering literature on AI applications in hospital management. Core biomedical databases include PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and MEDLINE [30]. Engineering and technology venues were added to broaden coverage: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and Google Scholar [58][41][64]. Additional grey‑literature repositories (CINAHL, EconLit, LILACS, African Index Medicus, CADTH Grey Matters, conference‑proceedings indices, and pre‑print servers such as arXiv) were searched to minimise publication bias [58].

The search was limited to English‑language records published between 2010 and 2025, a range that captures contemporary AI developments while maintaining relevance to current hospital practice [40]. A primary Boolean query was constructed to retrieve studies on AI‑driven scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow optimisation:  

```
("artificial intelligence" OR "AI" OR "machine learning" OR "ML" OR "deep learning" OR "reinforcement learning")
AND
("hospital scheduling" OR "resource allocation" OR "patient flow" OR "operational efficiency")
```  

This formulation derives from a concrete Boolean string reported for patient‑flow optimisation [2] and an expanded keyword set that includes terms such as “digital twin”, “predictive scheduling”, and “real‑time resource optimization” [104]. The same logical structure was translated for each database using platform‑specific syntax (e.g., MeSH terms for PubMed, TITLE‑ABS‑KEY for Scopus) in accordance with best‑practice translation guidelines [58].

Inclusion criteria required peer‑reviewed journal or conference papers that (i) apply at least one AI technique to hospital scheduling, resource allocation, or patient‑flow processes; (ii) report operational outcomes (e.g., wait‑time reduction, bed‑utilisation, cost savings); (iii) employ quantitative, qualitative, mixed‑methods, or economic‑evaluation designs; and (iv) are published in English from 2010 onward [73][30][40][41][2]. Studies focusing solely on AI models without a hospital‑management context, non‑English publications, opinion pieces, and pure methodological papers were excluded [41][90].

Title/abstract screening and full‑text eligibility assessment were conducted independently by two reviewers using Covidence software, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or a third reviewer. Duplicate records were removed with EndNote’s de‑duplication algorithm, and inter‑rater agreement was quantified using Cohen’s κ, targeting a minimum κ ≥ 0.80 for acceptable reliability [58][64].

Risk‑of‑bias and quality appraisal were performed with design‑specific tools: the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for randomized trials, ROBINS‑I for non‑randomized quantitative studies, the CHEERS checklist for economic evaluations, AMSTAR‑2 for existing systematic reviews, the CASP qualitative checklist and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for qualitative and mixed‑methods research, and the Newcastle‑Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case‑control studies [30][58][60][96].

Data were extracted using a structured form that captured all variables necessary to populate a technology‑impact matrix. The extraction fields are listed in Table 1.  

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| AI Technique | Algorithm or model used (e.g., deep learning, reinforcement learning) |
| Operational Domain | Scheduling, resource allocation, patient‑flow, bed‑utilisation, etc. |
| Outcome Metrics | Wait‑time reduction, throughput increase, cost savings, etc. |
| Effect Size | Quantitative impact with confidence intervals or odds ratios |
| Implementation Context | Hospital type, department, data source, integration level |
| Barriers / Facilitators | Reported challenges or enablers of implementation |
| Study Design | RCT, quasi‑experimental, simulation, economic evaluation |
| Funding / COI | Source of support and disclosed conflicts of interest |

The fields draw on extraction frameworks described for AI‑driven hospital studies and for structured data extraction using large language models [72][58][96][113].

Synthesis proceeded in three complementary stages. First, thematic mapping clustered studies by AI technique and operational domain, generating a visual overview of research foci [72][50]. Second, where ≥ 3 studies reported comparable quantitative effect sizes for a given outcome (e.g., average wait‑time reduction), a random‑effects meta‑analysis was performed, with heterogeneity assessed via the I² statistic; otherwise, a narrative aggregation of frequencies and descriptive statistics was provided [30][113]. Third, a technology‑impact matrix cross‑referenced AI methods with outcome domains and evidence strength (high, moderate, low) to highlight mature applications and evidence gaps, supporting decision‑makers in prioritising future investments [72][30][58].

Having established a rigorous, reproducible methodological foundation, the next section examines the AI‑enabled operational pillars—clinical and non‑clinical scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow optimisation.

# 3. AI‑Enabled Operational Pillars

AI technologies are reshaping the core operational pillars of hospitals by providing data‑driven decision support across scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow management. The first pillar examines AI‑enabled clinical and non‑clinical scheduling, highlighting predictive analytics and reinforcement‑learning approaches for appointments, operating‑room case sequencing, staff‑shift rostering, and equipment‑booking, together with real‑time rescheduling mechanisms. The second pillar addresses resource allocation and capacity management, covering AI‑driven bed and ICU planning, dynamic staffing, inventory optimisation, surge‑capacity planning, and predictive maintenance of critical assets. The third pillar explores patient‑flow optimisation, focusing on risk‑stratification models, logistics coordination, automated documentation, and discharge‑planning tools, all underpinned by interpretability and governance considerations. The following subsection delves into AI for clinical and non‑clinical scheduling.

## 3.1. AI for Clinical & Non‑Clinical Scheduling

AI‑driven scheduling systems are being deployed across both clinical and non‑clinical hospital functions to align patient demand, staff availability, and equipment capacity. By integrating predictive analytics, reinforcement‑learning (RL) policies, and real‑time rescheduling engines, these solutions aim to reduce idle resources, mitigate no‑show losses, and respect regulatory constraints while maintaining high‑quality care.

**Appointment and outpatient scheduling**  
Predictive classification models that estimate a patient’s probability of not attending an appointment have been embedded in commercial scheduling platforms. An AI‑based appointment system implemented in a Turkish university hospital used decision‑tree classifiers on demographic and historical attendance features to generate a no‑show risk score. The system automatically released high‑risk slots to a reserve pool and invited substitute patients within an hour, yielding a +10 % increase in attendance rates and a +6 % rise in physician capacity utilization over five months [4]. Similar approaches reported in a broader AI‑scheduling review achieved a reduction in MRI no‑show rates from 19.3 % to 15.9 % through XGBoost‑driven risk alerts [100]. A MAX‑RATE index policy combines estimated show‑up probabilities, clinical reward values, and an overtime cost parameter to prioritize appointments while respecting staffing caps, offering a simple, interpretable rule for overbooking and throughput maximisation [95].

**Operating‑room (OR) scheduling**  
Metaheuristic frameworks that incorporate RL components have produced near‑optimal makespan reductions for integrated OR problems. A biased random‑key genetic algorithm guided by Q‑learning (BRKGA‑QL) achieved an average relative percentile deviation of 0.05 % on benchmark instances and re‑optimised disrupted schedules within seconds, preserving fixed blocks and demonstrating feasibility for dynamic, real‑time rescheduling [112]. In a prospective study of an orthopedic joint‑replacement unit, a prescriptive analytics system predicted the likelihood of completing all four daily cases before a 3:45 pm cutoff and issued stage‑level alerts that projected a potential overtime cost saving of approximately $570 000 annually [21]. These decision models can be framed as Markov decision processes, where the system state captures resource availability, actions correspond to case sequencing, and rewards reflect objectives such as throughput and overtime penalties [97].

**Staff‑shift and workforce scheduling**  
Short‑term demand forecasts derived from stacked ensembles of gradient‑boosting, random‑forest, and ARIMA models have been used to align staffing rosters with anticipated emergency‑department surges, supporting compliance with the UK four‑hour target and bed‑occupancy thresholds [22]. RL approaches extend this capability by learning shift‑allocation policies that respect labor‑law limits. Deep Q‑network agents and policy‑gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization have been applied to simulated staff‑shift environments, enabling adaptive reassignment of nurses in response to stochastic patient arrivals [97]. A qualitative study of Swiss nurses highlighted the promise of RL for dynamic shift exchanges, although early graph‑neural‑network implementations failed to converge and underperformed mixed‑integer programming baselines [59]. Conversely, a deep‑RL scheduler evaluated across four community health clinics incorporated hard constraints on staffing ratios and budget caps, improving a composite treatment‑success score by 4.24 % relative to historical manual planning [98]. The MAX‑RATE index also offers a lightweight alternative that directly embeds overtime cost and capacity limits into the shift‑selection decision [95].

**Equipment‑booking and ancillary resource allocation**  
AI techniques that forecast equipment demand and embed constraint satisfaction have been reported in multiple domains. Multi‑objective optimisers consume transformer‑based demand forecasts to allocate imaging devices and infusion pumps, achieving 10–18 % higher utilisation while respecting labor and safety regulations [12]. A unified scheduling optimizer described in a digital‑health review combines time‑series forecasts with constraint solvers to generate equipment‑booking plans that automatically adjust to emergent clinical events [100].

**Predictive demand forecasting as a unifying layer**  
Accurate short‑term forecasts are prerequisite for all scheduling pillars. Machine‑learning pipelines that cluster historical demand patterns (e.g., K‑means) and apply support‑vector regression have attained mean absolute percentage errors below 2 % for one‑day inpatient bed forecasts, enabling proactive bed‑inventory reallocation and elective‑surgery adjustments [1]. Ensemble models that integrate weather, calendar effects, and Google flu search trends have similarly improved emergency‑department arrival predictions, informing both staff‑shift planning and OR case prioritisation [22]. A generic forecasting engine based on random‑forest and Gaussian‑process regression, as outlined in a synthesis of AI‑enabled operational pillars, provides the probabilistic inputs required by RL policies and optimisation solvers [65].

**Reinforcement‑learning schedulers across domains**  
The methodological landscape for RL in hospital scheduling includes tabular Q‑learning for patient boarding, approximate dynamic programming for high‑dimensional state spaces, and deep RL (DQN, DDPG, PPO) for continuous control of staff and equipment resources [97]. Hybrid approaches that couple RL with genetic algorithms or multi‑armed bandits have been proposed to balance exploration of novel schedules against exploitation of known high‑performing policies [11]. Empirical evidence from the BRKGA‑QL study demonstrates that RL‑guided parameter adaptation can reduce makespan by up to 7 % relative to conventional heuristics [112], while the deep‑RL staff‑resource allocator improves simulated treatment‑success scores without violating staffing ratios [98]. Nevertheless, many studies lack live‑deployment metrics, comprehensive cost‑benefit analyses, and explicit fairness assessments, highlighting areas for future investigation [65][59].

**Real‑time rescheduling mechanisms**  
Dynamic rescheduling is operationalised through event‑driven triggers that invoke optimisation or RL policies. The AI‑BAS appointment system detects a confirmed cancellation, releases the slot to a reserve pool, and dispatches an immediate invitation to a substitute patient, all within minutes [4]. In the OR domain, the BRKGA‑QL framework re‑optimises a partially cancelled surgery list (approximately 19 % cancellations) in seconds, preserving fixed time blocks and maintaining feasibility [112]. Prescriptive analytics for orthopedic surgery provides stage‑level alerts that recommend staffing or instrument adjustments in real time, projecting overtime reductions before they occur [21]. A four‑layer architecture described in the AIRA‑Hospital study integrates continuous demand forecasts, RL policy updates, and robotic‑process‑automation bots to execute schedule changes across appointments, ORs, staff rosters, and equipment bookings with sub‑second latency [12].

## 3.2. AI for Resource Allocation & Capacity Management

AI‑enabled resource allocation and capacity management encompass a suite of data‑driven techniques that transform how hospitals match beds, intensive‑care unit (ICU) slots, staff, consumables, and equipment to fluctuating patient demand. Central to this pillar are predictive analytics that forecast demand, optimisation engines that allocate scarce assets, and maintenance models that preserve equipment availability. The literature converges on five inter‑related sub‑domains: (1) bed and ICU capacity planning, (2) dynamic staffing, (3) inventory‑optimisation for consumables and equipment, (4) surge‑capacity planning, and (5) predictive maintenance of critical assets.

**Bed and ICU capacity planning**  
Machine‑learning (ML) and deep‑learning (DL) demand‑forecasting models ingest historical admissions, seasonal patterns, and real‑time clinical indicators to generate short‑term forecasts of inpatient and ICU load. Such forecasts underpin proactive bed‑allocation decisions and reduce occupancy bottlenecks. Ref57 documents the use of demand‑forecasting algorithms to enable anticipatory staffing and bed‑capacity planning, reporting improved forecasting accuracy and dynamic resource allocation as key benefits. Ref59 similarly describes a deep‑learning‑based time‑series fusion pipeline that predicts patient admissions and informs optimisation of bed and ICU assignments, thereby mitigating bottlenecks and enhancing patient flow. In a quantitative study of a UK hospital, a heuristic search combined with discrete‑event simulation reduced average queue size from 77.19 patients to 3.24 (≈ 96 % reduction) and total daily cost by ≈ 86 % through reconfiguration of bed clusters, demonstrating the operational impact of data‑driven allocation models even when the methodology is not explicitly labelled as “AI” [17].  

Reinforcement‑learning (RL) formulations cast the allocation problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) $M=\langle S,A,P,R,\gamma\rangle$, where states $S$ encode current bed occupancy and patient queues, actions $A$ represent admission or discharge decisions, transition probabilities $P$ model patient flow dynamics, and rewards $R$ penalise waiting time and resource under‑utilisation. Ref42 provides a comprehensive overview of model‑based and model‑free RL approaches for bed and ICU allocation, highlighting how queuing‑network RL can balance overflow costs against boarding times. Deep RL extensions, such as the joint‑patient TxDDQN architecture, directly allocate ICU beds and ventilators while managing the combinatorial explosion of the action space; the individual‑patient DDQN variant offers a tractable alternative that still yields fair and efficient resource distribution [13].

**Dynamic staffing**  
Accurate demand forecasts feed directly into staffing models that adjust shift rosters in near real‑time. Ref57 notes that demand forecasting “enables anticipatory staffing” and that dynamic resource allocation can adjust staff assignments as demand fluctuates. Ref59 reports AI‑driven dynamic staffing pipelines that process electronic health‑record (EHR) data and staff‑preference inputs to generate workforce‑need forecasts and automatically match required skillsets to scheduled tasks. Reinforcement‑learning policies further enhance adaptability: model‑free RL agents learn optimal staffing levels under stochastic patient arrivals, while policy‑gradient methods (e.g., PPO) handle continuous staffing adjustments with labour‑cost constraints [97]. The stochastic epidemic‑driven DP framework described in Ref69 produces timing and magnitude recommendations for capacity expansion, implicitly guiding staffing scale‑up during pandemic surges and quantifying the marginal cost of additional staff via shadow pricing.

**Inventory‑optimisation for consumables and equipment**  
AI‑enhanced inventory‑optimisation combines demand forecasts with optimisation algorithms to minimise stock‑outs and excess holding costs. Ref57 identifies dynamic reorder‑point and safety‑stock calculations as core AI tools for consumables and equipment parts. Ref72 demonstrates that AI‑driven allocation models achieve operational cost reductions while improving staffing levels, equipment utilisation, and bed/ICU planning; the study attributes these gains to more accurate demand forecasts that drive just‑in‑time ordering. Stochastic optimisation models, such as the chance‑constrained programming (CCP) formulation in Ref68, explicitly incorporate LOS uncertainty into bed‑assignment decisions, thereby aligning inventory replenishment with probabilistic patient turnover. **Surge‑capacity planning**  
AI models that fuse clinical, environmental, and social‑vulnerability data enable early warning of demand spikes. Ref76 outlines how ML/DL pipelines predict heat‑related or asthma‑exacerbation admissions, allowing hospitals to pre‑emptively open additional ICU bays and reallocate staff. Ref85 extends this concept with climate‑health digital twins that integrate sensor feeds and AI forecasts to trigger surge‑capacity protocols before patient influx peaks. The stochastic epidemic‑driven optimisation in Ref69 provides a systematic method for determining when to expand ICU capacity, balancing budget constraints against the expected number of denied patients. These approaches translate probabilistic forecasts into actionable capacity‑expansion policies, reducing reliance on static, reactive protocols.

**Predictive maintenance of critical equipment**  
Maintaining ventilators, dialysis machines, HVAC systems, and other life‑supporting devices is essential for uninterrupted care delivery. Sensor‑driven ML models detect early signs of component degradation, enabling condition‑based maintenance schedules that lower unexpected downtime. Ref74’s systematic review of AI‑enabled predictive maintenance in hospital facilities highlights remote monitoring, real‑time anomaly detection, and labour‑saving functions as core capabilities, albeit without quantitative impact metrics. The digital‑twin framework described in Ref85 proposes embedding AI‑based degradation models within a real‑time simulation environment, allowing hospitals to schedule maintenance proactively based on predicted failure probabilities. Reinforcement‑learning formulations, as discussed in Ref42, can further optimise maintenance actions by treating equipment health states as MDP states and learning policies that minimise total expected downtime and maintenance cost.

**Data foundations**  
Effective AI‑driven allocation relies on high‑quality, timely data streams. Core sources include EHRs, historical admission and utilisation records, real‑time equipment logs, staffing rosters, and external indicators such as epidemiological reports or climate data (Ref29, Ref45, Ref83). Knowledge‑graph architectures facilitate the integration of heterogeneous datasets, providing a unified substrate for AI models to query during allocation decisions (Ref83). Challenges such as data silos, heterogeneity, and privacy constraints are repeatedly noted (Ref57, Ref45, Ref85), underscoring the need for interoperable data platforms and robust governance.

**Implementation challenges and governance**  
Across the reviewed literature, recurring barriers include data quality and integration issues, talent shortages, change‑management hurdles, and regulatory compliance (Ref57, Ref45). Ethical considerations—algorithmic transparency, bias audits, and human‑in‑the‑loop oversight—are highlighted as prerequisites for trustworthy deployment (Ref59, Ref70, Ref85). Governance frameworks that embed explainability, accountability, and continuous performance monitoring are essential to ensure that AI‑driven allocation decisions align with clinical safety standards and institutional policies.

In summary, AI techniques ranging from demand‑forecasting ML models to reinforcement‑learning and stochastic optimisation provide a cohesive toolbox for allocating beds, ICU capacity, staff, consumables, and equipment while anticipating surges and preserving equipment reliability. The quantitative evidence—from queue‑size reductions of > 95 % and cost savings of up to 91 % in bed‑allocation studies [17] to early‑warning gains in climate‑driven surge models [51]—demonstrates the transformative potential of AI in hospital capacity management.  

Having examined AI for resource allocation and capacity management, the following section will explore how AI can further improve patient‑flow optimisation across the hospital enterprise.

## 3.3. AI for Patient‑Flow Optimization

AI‑enabled patient‑flow optimization seeks to harmonise every step of the admission‑to‑discharge continuum by anticipating congestion, automating information exchange, and directing logistical resources in real time. The literature converges on four inter‑related capabilities: (i) predictive risk stratification that flags patients likely to generate downstream bottlenecks; (ii) analytics‑driven coordination of intra‑hospital logistics such as transport and diagnostics; (iii) automated documentation and discharge‑planning support; and (iv) closed‑loop feedback systems that translate patient‑experience data into operational adjustments.

Predictive risk models are deployed at the point of admission to generate patient‑level scores for outcomes that directly shape flow, most notably 30‑day readmission, prolonged length of stay (LOS), and early in‑hospital mortality. An AI‑based clinical decision‑support platform evaluated all admissions in real time and produced a readmission risk score that, when coupled with automated discharge‑planning alerts, achieved a 25 % relative reduction in 30‑day readmissions (absolute drop from 11.4 % to 8.1 %) with a sensitivity of 65 % and specificity of 89 % (NNT = 11) [52]. Systematic reviews of machine‑learning readmission predictors corroborate these gains: random‑forest and gradient‑boosting models improved AUC by 17–23 % over logistic regression, and hybrid human‑plus‑machine workflows reduced COPD readmissions by 48 % in the high‑risk subgroup [16]. A separate study of personalized outcome prediction reported AUCs of 0.76 for readmission, 0.84 for LOS > 5 days, and 0.91 for early death, demonstrating that calibrated probabilities can be used to prioritise admissions, allocate beds, and trigger early interventions [93].

Beyond risk scores, AI analytics are applied to intra‑hospital logistics that have traditionally been sources of delay. Kropp et al. constructed a two‑stage pipeline comprising a multilayer‑perceptron (MLP) that predicts the hourly count of delayed transports and a genetic‑algorithm optimiser that reallocates transport staff and automation settings. The predictive model attained an adjusted $R^{2}=0.794$ and, when coupled with the optimiser, reduced predicted delayed transports by 27 % using only staff‑reallocation and by up to 42 % when transport volume and automation rate were jointly adjusted [19]. Parallel evidence from a broad AI‑operational review shows that predictive models of admissions, discharges, and transfers achieve 86–93 % accuracy in wait‑time forecasts, while AI‑driven scheduling reduced outpatient MRI no‑show rates from 19.3 % to 15.9 % (≈ 3.4 pp absolute reduction) and lowered overall waiting‑time and overtime costs by 15–40 % in an infusion‑unit case study [100].

Automation of clinical documentation further accelerates flow by shortening the latency between diagnostic imaging and actionable information. Natural‑language‑processing (NLP)‑enabled triage systems scan electronic health records to flag urgent studies, and AI‑augmented reporting tools automatically extract key imaging findings and populate structured EHR fields. One implementation shortened radiology report delivery from 11.2 days to 2.7 days, a 76 % reduction that directly speeds downstream treatment decisions and discharge planning [2]. Robotic‑process‑automation (RPA) programs complement these gains by updating patient records, processing prior‑authorisation requests, and handling revenue‑cycle tasks, thereby reducing manual turnaround times across the admission‑to‑discharge pathway [89].

Discharge planning benefits from AI models that predict destination, expected LOS, and daily discharge volume. Machine‑learning ensembles (random forest, XGBoost, artificial neural networks) routinely attain up to 92 % accuracy for home‑discharge prediction and outperform clinician judgment in early‑discharge ranking [48]. These forecasts are integrated into EHR dashboards that trigger alerts for case‑management teams, enabling proactive scheduling of post‑acute services, medication reconciliation, and follow‑up appointments. The same integration strategy was described in a recent AI‑powered readmission‑risk deployment, where real‑time risk scores displayed on a clinician dashboard prompted automated alerts, SHAP‑based explanations of contributing variables, and immediate initiation of transitional‑care interventions [63].

Interpretability and governance are recurring themes across the evidence base. Interpretable Generalized Additive Neural Networks (IGANN) provide shape‑plot visualisations of feature contributions, allowing operations managers to understand how variables such as occupancy level or staffing ratios drive bottleneck risk while supporting bias mitigation through feature‑level adjustments [53]. Regulatory and ethical frameworks from the FDA, European Commission, and professional organisations emphasise transparency, fairness, and post‑market monitoring for AI tools that influence patient flow, underscoring the need for explainable models and audit trails [6].

Nonetheless, implementation barriers persist, including EHR integration challenges, data‑quality constraints, and the necessity for regulatory‑compliant explainability [6][89]. Addressing these barriers through interoperable data platforms, robust governance structures, and transparent model design will be essential for scaling patient‑flow optimisation across heterogeneous hospital systems.

# 4. Emerging AI Technologies & Integration

This section surveys recent AI advances that can augment the three operational pillars of hospital management. It first examines generative AI—large‑language and diffusion models—for decision support, protocol and workflow generation, and natural‑language scheduling interfaces. ## 4.1. Generative AI for Decision Support & Process Design

Large‑language models (LLMs) and diffusion‑based generative models have emerged as versatile tools for hospital decision support and process design. Their ability to ingest heterogeneous clinical data, generate coherent textual artefacts, and interact with existing information systems positions them as core components for scenario simulation, protocol generation, natural‑language scheduling interfaces, and automated workflow construction.

**Scenario simulation and protocol generation**  
LLMs can transform free‑text clinician inputs into structured clinical pathways, order sets, and patient‑flow simulations. The ScienceDirect review documents how multimodal LLMs ingest text, images, audio, and video to produce draft protocols and dynamic scenario models for care delivery [39]. Empirical work on resource‑allocation LLMs demonstrated automated generation of operating‑room schedules and postoperative‑bed assignments that respected multi‑objective criteria and outperformed traditional exact and heuristic methods in a French hospital pilot [111]. Similar capabilities are reported for GPT‑4‑based systems that generate pre‑operative instructions with 91.4 % accuracy, surpassing human clinicians (86.3 %) in a 14‑scenario study [94]. Across reviews, LLMs are repeatedly highlighted as enablers of rapid protocol drafting and scenario synthesis, yet quantitative impact metrics (e.g., length‑of‑stay reduction) remain scarce [24][31].

**Natural‑language interfacing with scheduling systems**  
Conversely, LLMs serve as conversational front‑ends to electronic health‑record (EHR) scheduling modules. They translate clinician‑level language into structured appointment slots, resource‑allocation directives, and revenue‑cycle actions, effectively acting as natural‑language agents for hospital operations [39]. In a comparative evaluation of scheduling‑explanation LLMs, GPT‑4 achieved higher correctness (mean 0.67) and comparable completeness (> 0.94) across swap, increase, and decrease query types, supporting reliable bidirectional interaction with scheduling engines [27]. Retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) pipelines further enrich NL queries with up‑to‑date guideline knowledge, enabling real‑time decision support for “what‑if” staffing or bed‑availability scenarios [111][94].

**Automated workflow design**  
The generation of executable workflow specifications can be driven by LLM‑produced textual artefacts that are subsequently mapped onto formal workflow management system (WfMS) models. The literature on process‑oriented health information systems outlines a taxonomy of clinical guidelines, protocols, and care pathways that must be respected when automating workflows [35]. SWEL, a domain‑specific modeling language, offers a platform‑independent metamodel that can be populated by LLM‑generated specifications, enabling seamless translation into executable graphs for scheduling, bed allocation, or ICU staffing [34]. Moreover, LLMs have been shown to automate documentation pipelines (e.g., discharge summaries, ICU notes) and to generate synthetic training data that accelerates downstream workflow models [24].

**Diffusion models as visual scenario simulators**  
Diffusion models provide a probabilistic generative framework capable of synthesising high‑fidelity visual data. Core mechanisms—denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs), score‑based generative models (SGMs), and stochastic differential equations (SDEs)—are well‑documented in health‑informatics surveys [92]. While most applications to date focus on image‑centric tasks (anomaly detection, segmentation, super‑resolution), the underlying reverse‑process generation could be repurposed to visualise patient‑flow bottlenecks or resource‑utilisation heatmaps. Current literature, however, does not present concrete deployments of diffusion models for hospital‑operational scenario simulation, highlighting a clear research gap [92][24].

**Customization and integration pathways**  
Effective deployment of generative AI in hospital settings requires model tailoring and robust interoperability. Four customization strategies are identified for LLMs: prompt engineering, retrieval‑augmented generation, fine‑tuning on institution‑specific data, and agentic augmentation that enables autonomous API calls to bed‑management or scheduling services [39]. Multimodal extensions (e.g., Med‑Flamingo, LLaVA‑Med) further expand the input space to include imaging and vital‑sign streams, supporting richer scenario generation [24]. Interoperability standards such as HL7 FHIR and SMART on FHIR are advocated as the primary data‑exchange layer for integrating LLM‑driven services with existing EHR and resource‑allocation APIs [54]. The situation‑awareness (SA) framework for real‑time simulation underscores the need for AI outputs to support perception, comprehension, and projection levels, guiding the design of NL interfaces that convey certainty and actionable recommendations [31].

**Pilot design with compliance and KPI targets**  
Building on these capabilities, a short‑term pilot can be implemented in a high‑throughput outpatient clinic to evaluate an LLM‑driven scheduling interface. The pilot would employ prompt‑engineered queries that map clinician requests (e.g., “Schedule a follow‑up for patient X next week”) to structured appointment slots via a FHIR‑based API, with a human‑in‑the‑loop reviewer confirming each generated schedule before execution. Compliance items include encryption of all data in transit and at rest, adherence to GDPR/HIPAA privacy safeguards, and documentation of model provenance through audit logs, reflecting the regulatory considerations highlighted for LLM deployment [39]. Performance would be measured against explicit KPI thresholds: scheduling latency ≤ 5 minutes per request, and a ≥ 10 % reduction in no‑show rate compared with the baseline manual process, targets consistent with reported improvements in appointment adherence from AI‑enabled scheduling studies [39][24]. Success criteria would require both KPI attainment and a documented bias audit demonstrating no disproportionate impact across protected patient groups.

**Evaluation, explainability, and governance**  
Quantitative evaluation of LLM‑based scheduling assistance is emerging. The scheduling‑explanation study reported GPT‑4 correctness improvements of ≈ 60 % over baseline and high semantic alignment (BERTScore ≈ 0.65), providing an evidence base for technology‑impact matrices that link model performance to operational outcomes [27]. Explainability techniques—counterfactual explanations, model‑cards, and SHAP‑based feature attribution—are proposed to mitigate hallucinations and bias, which remain prominent limitations of generative AI in clinical contexts [39][27]. Governance considerations include compliance with the EU AI Act (high‑risk classification for healthcare AI) and FDA pathways for internal AI tools, alongside industry‑led assurance labs that validate safety and fairness before hospital deployment [54].

**Evidence gaps and research priorities**  
Despite promising capabilities, the literature lacks peer‑reviewed studies that quantify the downstream impact of LLM‑generated scheduling protocols on key performance indicators such as length‑of‑stay, overtime costs, or readmission rates [54][24]. Real‑time validation of diffusion‑model visualisations for operational decision‑making is also absent. Future work should (i) conduct controlled pilots that embed LLM‑driven scenario simulation within live scheduling engines, (ii) evaluate diffusion‑model visual analytics against clinician decision quality, and (iii) integrate robust XAI and human‑in‑the‑loop validation loops to satisfy regulatory and ethical requirements [54][31].

## 4.2. Federated Learning for Privacy‑Preserving Multi‑Hospital Models

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a decentralized training paradigm that enables hospitals to jointly develop AI models for scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow optimisation while keeping raw patient data on‑site, thereby satisfying privacy regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR [8]. In the canonical FL workflow, a central orchestrator distributes the current global model to participating institutions; each site performs local optimisation on its private dataset and returns only model updates (e.g., gradients or weight deltas). The orchestrator aggregates these updates—typically via weighted averaging (FedAvg)—to produce an improved global model, which is then redistributed for the next training round [8].

Beyond the classic hub‑and‑spoke design, several alternative topologies have been proposed to reduce reliance on a single server and to accommodate legal or network constraints. Peer‑to‑peer schemes allow nodes to exchange updates directly, while hierarchical arrangements introduce intermediate aggregation layers (e.g., regional clusters) before a top‑level consolidation. Hybrid configurations combine elements of both approaches to balance scalability, latency, and trust requirements [102][14]. Table 1 summarises the principal FL topologies and the privacy‑enhancing mechanisms commonly coupled with each.

| Topology | Coordination Model | Typical Privacy Enhancements |
|---|---|---|
| Centralised (hub‑&‑spoke) | Single server aggregates all client updates | Secure aggregation, differential privacy (DP) |
| Peer‑to‑peer | Direct client‑client exchange, no permanent server | Ledger‑based audit (e.g., Biscotti), DP, homomorphic encryption (HE) |
| Hierarchical | Sub‑federations aggregate locally before a global step | Secure aggregation at each layer, DP, optional blockchain for traceability |
| Hybrid | Mix of central and peer links, dynamic leader rotation | Combination of the above (secure aggregation + DP + blockchain) [102][14] |

**Privacy‑preserving augmentations** are essential because model updates can still leak sensitive information. Differential privacy adds calibrated noise to gradients, providing formal ε‑DP guarantees; secure aggregation encrypts updates so that the aggregator never sees individual contributions; homomorphic encryption enables computation on encrypted parameters; and blockchain‑based smart contracts furnish immutable audit trails for update provenance [102][14][70][37].

Empirical evidence demonstrates that FL can achieve predictive performance comparable to centrally trained models for tasks directly relevant to hospital operations. Multi‑site readmission‑risk models built on electronic health‑record (EHR) data reported area‑under‑the‑receiver‑operating‑characteristic (AUROC) scores of 0.82–0.83, only marginally below the centralized baseline of 0.83 [8]. Similar parity was observed for in‑hospital mortality prediction across intensive‑care units and for length‑of‑stay forecasting using the eICU collaborative dataset, with FL models matching centralized counterparts while preserving data locality [8].

A concrete multi‑hospital case study from three tertiary hospitals in Nigeria illustrates the practical feasibility of FL for a resource‑allocation‑related outcome. The collaboration trained a FedAvg model to predict 30‑day readmission risk using 15 200 de‑identified records that included bed‑capacity metadata. The federated model attained an AUROC of 0.82 (vs. 0.83 for a centralized multilayer perceptron) and identical accuracy (0.75), while each local model’s AUROC improved from 0.75–0.78 to 0.81–0.82 under FL—a 3–7 % gain without any raw data exchange [29]. Communication overhead amounted to 38 MB per round over 50 rounds, and training time increased modestly from 42 min (centralized) to 48 min (federated). Privacy was quantified by a reduction in membership‑inference attack success from 22 % to 8 %, confirming the protective effect of model‑parameter encryption [29]. Governance was ensured through institutional review board approvals, data‑use agreements, and hospital‑level data‑sharing policies [29].

The DeCaPH framework further showcases how advanced privacy techniques can be integrated into a decentralized FL system without sacrificing utility. Across three real‑world tasks (mortality prediction, single‑cell classification, chest‑radiology pathology identification), the DP‑augmented FL pipeline incurred less than 3.2 % performance loss relative to a non‑privacy collaborative baseline, while membership‑inference vulnerability decreased by up to 16 % [70]. Governance mechanisms included random leader selection—replacing a permanent server—to enhance transparency and auditability, and secure aggregation to prevent any leader from inspecting individual updates [70].

Despite these promising results, a systematic review of FL in healthcare highlights that only 5.2 % of identified studies reported real‑world clinical deployments, with the majority remaining proof‑of‑concept prototypes [45]. Key barriers specific to hospital‑management contexts include: (i) statistical heterogeneity across sites that hampers convergence; (ii) system heterogeneity (varying compute resources and network bandwidth); (iii) residual privacy leakage from model updates; (iv) communication overhead for deep‑learning models; (v) lack of incentive and intellectual‑property frameworks to motivate data‑rich institutions; and (vi) a paucity of health‑economic evaluations quantifying downstream cost savings or LOS reductions [45].

The literature on FL for operational hospital tasks provides concrete algorithmic contributions that can be mapped to scheduling and resource‑allocation challenges. Client‑scheduling algorithms such as the online selection method by Xu B et al. (2021) and the multi‑armed bandit approach by Xia W et al. (2020) reduce latency and improve convergence speed, directly informing how hospitals might schedule FL participants (e.g., departments or regional sites) to train shared staffing or bed‑allocation models [37]. Hierarchical edge‑association and resource‑allocation schemes (e.g., HFEL by Luo S et al., 2020) optimise bandwidth and compute distribution, offering a blueprint for allocating IT resources across a hospital network [37]. Fog‑enabled FL platforms such as FedHealthFog and blockchain‑assisted variants (BLADE‑FL, Akhmetov A et al., 2025) further demonstrate how secure, low‑latency analytics can support near‑real‑time patient‑flow monitoring [37].

**Challenges specific to hospital‑management FL deployments** remain pronounced. Data heterogeneity—differences in patient demographics, coding practices, and operational metrics—violates the independent‑and‑identically‑distributed (IID) assumption, potentially degrading model convergence and necessitating algorithmic mitigations such as FedProx or personalization [8][45]. Trust levels vary across consortia; while trusted networks can rely on legal agreements, less‑trusted federations require robust cryptographic safeguards (DP, secure aggregation, blockchain) to mitigate insider attacks [14][70]. Traceability demands comprehensive logging of training configurations and hyper‑parameters to satisfy regulatory audits [14]. Finally, participating hospitals must provision on‑premise compute capable of local model training and secure communication, a prerequisite that may strain resource‑constrained sites [14][45].

**Future research priorities** align with the identified gaps. First, real‑world pilot studies should apply FL to explicit scheduling, staff‑rostering, and operating‑room allocation problems, reporting quantitative outcomes such as % reduction in overtime or improvement in bed‑turnover. Second, advanced privacy‑preserving techniques (e.g., differential‑privacy budgets calibrated for operational models, homomorphic‑encryption‑enabled updates) need systematic evaluation of the accuracy‑privacy trade‑off in high‑frequency training scenarios. Third, health‑economic analyses must quantify cost‑benefit ratios, ROI, and LOS impacts of FL‑driven decision support. Fourth, governance frameworks should formalise incentive structures, IP sharing, and multi‑institutional oversight committees to sustain long‑term collaboration. Finally, standardized FL pipelines tailored to hospital operational data (time‑series admission logs, staffing rosters) will improve reproducibility and accelerate translation from proof‑of‑concept to production [8][45][37].

## 4.3. Real‑Time Digital Twins of Hospital Operations

Real‑time digital twins (DTs) are virtual replicas of hospital processes that are continuously synchronised with operational data streams, thereby forming a closed‑loop symbiotic simulation where the physical system and its digital counterpart exchange information in near‑real time[31].  Within this paradigm the twin incorporates physics‑informed models—such as discrete‑event simulation (DES), system‑dynamics equations, or agent‑based representations—that encode the mechanistic dynamics of patient flow, resource utilisation, and staffing interactions, while data‑driven components refine model parameters as new observations arrive[46][83].  The overarching architecture typically comprises (i) a data‑integration layer that ingests heterogeneous feeds from electronic health records, IoT sensors, and equipment logs via standards such as FHIR, HL7, or OMOP; (ii) a simulation engine that executes the physics‑informed model; (iii) a visualisation and decision‑support interface that presents descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics; and (iv) a feedback loop in which recommendations are enacted by human operators and the resulting system state is fed back to update the twin[31][9][78].  This structure enables the twin to act as a “situational‑awareness” platform, supporting Level 1 perception, Level 2 comprehension, and Level 3 projection of hospital performance[31].

Descriptive analytics provide an up‑to‑date snapshot of current occupancy, equipment status, and staff availability, while diagnostic analytics highlight the root causes of observed bottlenecks (e.g., mismatched bed‑to‑patient ratios).  Predictive analytics forecast near‑future demand for beds, ventilators, or imaging slots using time‑series or physics‑based extrapolation, and prescriptive analytics generate optimal “what‑if” intervention sets—such as adding a staff shift, re‑configuring a ward, or expanding ICU capacity—by solving optimisation problems of the form  
$$\min_{x\in\Theta} g(x)$$  
where $g(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y(x,n)]$ denotes the expected performance metric (e.g., average wait time) of decision vector $x$[46].  The twin therefore serves as a decision‑support engine that can evaluate multiple scenarios instantly, without exposing patients to risk.

Several real‑world deployments illustrate the operational impact of such twins.  GE Healthcare’s “Care Command” command‑center twin integrated statewide bed‑ and ventilator‑capacity data during the COVID‑19 pandemic, maximising critical‑care utilisation and enabling early detection of patient deterioration[74].  Siemens Healthineers, in partnership with the Medical University of South Carolina, employed a twin to simulate workflow and equipment changes, informing real‑time scheduling adjustments even though quantitative KPI values were not disclosed[74].  An AnyLogic‑based twin for two NHS Foundation Trust hospitals captured discrete‑event patient pathways and agent‑based behaviour, producing detailed reports on ward‑level bed occupancy, queue lengths, and emergency‑department throughput, and supporting winter‑demand stress‑testing[42].  The Mater Hospital digital‑twin reduced average CT/MRI wait times by 240 minutes and increased scanner capacity by 32 % (CT) and 26 % (MRI) through dynamic workflow simulation[18].  In the JUNEAU emergency‑department twin, a synchronised DT combined real‑time data with a DES engine to forecast “emergency throughput time” and to switch between digital‑shadow, synchronised, and exploratory modes for rapid scenario generation[68].  A cloud‑native critical‑care twin built on Azure demonstrated a 72 % real‑time capture rate of staff‑performed tasks and reported service‑quality improvements of 17–22 % in a related emergency‑communication centre study[36].  Finally, a multi‑hospital study of four peer‑reviewed implementations reported a 25 % increase in surgical throughput after twin deployment at Karolinska University Hospital[3].

Physics‑informed modelling is central to these implementations.  DES encodes stochastic patient‑entity events and resource constraints, while system‑dynamics formulations capture aggregate stock‑and‑flow relationships such as bed occupancy versus admission rates[46].  Hybrid approaches combine demand‑forecast modules with DES engines, as in the endoscopy‑service twin that couples Poisson‑distributed arrival forecasts with a Simul8 DES to evaluate six‑day‑week or 99 % utilisation scenarios[44].  Physics‑informed machine learning (PIML) further augments traditional simulators by embedding governing equations (e.g., fluid dynamics for ventilator airflow) into neural networks, reducing data requirements and accelerating surrogate‑model evaluation for near‑real‑time what‑if analysis[83][107][38].  High‑performance computing platforms such as NVIDIA Omniverse “Mega” blueprint enable large‑scale, multi‑agent twins that can simulate whole‑hospital ecosystems with sub‑second latency[83].

What‑if capabilities are routinely leveraged for capacity planning, patient‑flow optimisation, and resource allocation.  The “shadow twin” concept continuously calibrates the virtual model against live streams, allowing instantaneous projection of demand spikes and evaluation of mitigation strategies such as additional staffing or bed re‑allocation[74].  Integration with Planning & Scheduling 4.0 (P&S 4.0) tools supplies high‑quality schedule inputs that the twin can ingest; a case study in automotive manufacturing demonstrated a 24 % reduction in projected colour‑change bottlenecks when P&S 4.0 schedules were coupled with a DT, a principle directly translatable to hospital OR or ICU scheduling[77].  Digital‑twin platforms also support scenario exploration across multiple horizons: a synchronised DT can run accelerated forecasts for seasonal influenza surges, while an exploratory DT permits extensive policy testing without affecting live operations[68].  The resulting decision support can be expressed as prescriptive recommendations (e.g., “increase ICU staffing by 15 % during weeks 3–5”) that are fed back to administrators via interactive dashboards[9].

Validation frameworks remain a critical research focus.  The “continuous shadow‑twin” approach advocates ongoing comparison of simulated outputs with observed KPIs—such as bed‑turnover rate, equipment‑utilisation, or patient‑throughput time—and iterative model recalibration when deviation thresholds are exceeded[74][78].  Reported performance metrics include service‑quality gains of 17–22 % in emergency‑communication centres[36], a 25 % surgical‑throughput increase at Karolinska[3], and a 24 % reduction in colour‑change bottlenecks in the manufacturing analogue[77].  However, many deployments lack formal statistical validation (e.g., prediction‑error quantification, confidence intervals) and do not link twin outputs to predefined operational KPIs, limiting the ability to assess causal impact[55][105].

The literature also highlights several persistent challenges.  Interoperability is hindered by heterogeneous data formats; while standards such as FHIR and HL7 are frequently recommended, concrete implementations that achieve seamless, bi‑directional data exchange at hospital scale are scarce[74][9].  Real‑time data acquisition from legacy EHRs, bedside monitors, and IoT devices remains technically demanding and often incurs substantial integration overhead[5][55].  Validation gaps—particularly the absence of rigorous, peer‑reviewed impact studies that report cost‑effectiveness, ROI, or long‑term patient‑outcome metrics—are repeatedly noted as research priorities[74][3][105].  Governance, ethical, and security considerations—including encryption, role‑based access, audit trails, and compliance with HIPAA/GDPR—are discussed in multiple sources but rarely operationalised in published case studies[74][18].  Finally, privacy‑preserving techniques such as federated learning have been proposed for multi‑institutional twin training, yet concrete hospital‑level implementations remain under‑reported[74][18].

In summary, dynamic, physics‑informed digital twins provide a versatile, data‑driven foundation for real‑time hospital management.  They enable descriptive visibility, diagnostic insight, predictive forecasting, and prescriptive optimisation within a single, continuously updated platform.  While early deployments demonstrate promising operational gains, the field requires stronger standards for data interoperability, rigorous validation protocols, economic evaluation, and governance frameworks to transition from prototype to evidence‑based, scalable solutions.

# 6. Implementation Barriers, Ethics, & Governance

Implementation of AI‑driven hospital‑management tools encounters a constellation of technical, organizational, regulatory, and ethical barriers that must be addressed in an integrated manner.  

**Technical barriers** – Hospital AI pipelines process large volumes of protected health information, making compliance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) a baseline requirement; this entails encryption at rest and in transit, strict role‑based access controls, and continuous cybersecurity monitoring [87][28][81][109]. Even when raw data remain on‑site, model‑update exchanges in federated‑learning (FL) frameworks can leak sensitive information; mitigation strategies such as differential privacy, secure aggregation, and homomorphic encryption are recommended to bound privacy loss while preserving predictive utility [70][109][85]. Algorithmic bias is another technical obstacle: models trained on historical electronic health‑record (EHR) data may inherit disparities that produce inequitable recommendations for under‑served groups; the literature stresses inclusive data collection, bias‑aware algorithm design, and continuous fairness monitoring throughout the AI lifecycle [28][26][79][108]. Interoperability challenges arise from heterogeneous legacy systems and non‑standard data formats; without standardized interfaces (e.g., HL7 FHIR, SNOMED‑CT, LOINC) and semantic metadata registries, AI pipelines cannot reliably ingest or output the information required for real‑time scheduling and resource‑allocation decisions [87][66][103].  

**Organizational barriers** – Successful AI integration requires coordinated change‑management processes. Multidisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, data scientists, ethicists, and health‑information‑management (HIM) professionals is a prerequisite for aligning technical solutions with clinical workflows [66][ref27bb2073]. Skill gaps in AI literacy, resistance to workflow redesign, and the need for sustained staff training further impede adoption [66][ref27bb2073]. Reimbursement structures often lag behind technological innovation; hospitals lack clear billing pathways that recognise AI‑enabled services, creating uncertainty about return‑on‑investment and limiting capital allocation for AI projects [28][76].  

**Regulatory barriers** – GDPR and HIPAA provide foundational data‑protection frameworks, but the forthcoming EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) classifies many hospital‑AI applications as “high‑risk,” imposing mandatory documentation of model architecture, training‑data provenance, and post‑market surveillance [7][79]. In the United States, the FDA’s proposed AI/ML Software‑as‑a‑Medical‑Device (SaMD) guidance requires real‑world performance monitoring and bias reporting, yet specific guidance for operational tools such as scheduling algorithms remains limited [7][25]. Canadian guidance similarly calls for adaptive regulatory pathways that accommodate iterative learning systems [66]. Liability allocation is ambiguous; regulators and institutions must delineate responsibility among AI developers, vendors, and clinicians to avoid legal uncertainty [28][7][25].  

**Ethical barriers** – Autonomy and human‑in‑the‑loop (HITL) oversight are emphasized to preserve clinician judgment and patient agency, especially for high‑stakes decisions such as triage or operating‑room scheduling [28][79][108]. Transparency and explainability are required to build trust; clinicians need intelligible rationales for AI recommendations, and patients must receive clear information about AI involvement in their care [28][79][108]. Justice and fairness demand that bias mitigation be embedded in design and that performance be reported across demographic sub‑groups [26][79][108]. Public engagement and participatory governance are highlighted as mechanisms for societal trust and inclusive decision‑making [28][79].  

**Standards and governance frameworks** – A layered governance approach can translate high‑level ethical principles into concrete, auditable controls. The OECD AI Principles and UNESCO Recommendation provide rights‑based foundations (inclusive growth, respect for law and human rights, transparency, robustness, accountability) that inform hospital‑level policies [76]. Operational guidance is supplied by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), which structures governance into four functions—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—and prescribes continuous testing, monitoring, and risk mitigation [76]. ISO/IEC 42001 (ISO 42001) offers a certifiable AI Management System (AIMS) based on a Plan‑Do‑Check‑Act cycle, enabling hospitals to demonstrate “reasonable care” to regulators and payers [76]. IEEE 7000‑2021 provides a five‑step process for embedding stakeholder values (including privacy, fairness, and accountability) into system design, with a formal Value Register that traces ethical requirements to technical specifications [76]. Sector‑specific adaptations such as the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI) and the Joint Commission’s AI certification align these generic frameworks with hospital accreditation and Medicare reimbursement requirements [76].

**Risk‑mitigation strategies** – Building on the matrix, hospitals should (i) embed GDPR/HIPAA‑by‑design de‑identification and DP mechanisms in the data‑handling pipeline; (ii) institute regular bias‑impact assessments and update the bias‑audit report whenever new data are incorporated; (iii) adopt FHIR‑compliant interfaces and a metadata registry to achieve semantic interoperability; (iv) enforce HITL checkpoints with documented clinician sign‑off; (v) maintain immutable audit trails for all model updates and decision events; and (vi) establish multidisciplinary AI governance committees that oversee compliance with ISO 42001, IEEE 7000, and CHAI/TJC certification requirements [76][66]. Continuous post‑deployment monitoring—aligned with the AI RMF “Measure” and ISO 42001 “Check” functions—should track KPI drift, bias metrics, and security incidents, triggering corrective actions in the AI RMF “Manage” and ISO 42001 “Act” phases.  

Having mapped the technical, organizational, regulatory, and ethical hurdles together with concrete governance and mitigation frameworks, the next section evaluates the reported outcomes, evaluation metrics, and impact evidence associated with AI‑enabled hospital‑management interventions.

# 7. Integrated Evidence‑Based Future‑Direction Roadmap

The Integrated Evidence‑Based Future‑Direction Roadmap synthesizes operational pillars, emerging AI technologies, outcome metrics, and governance considerations into a phased implementation strategy. The short‑term segment ($1$–$2$ years) outlines low‑effort pilots such as predictive scheduling, resource‑allocation dashboards, and an initial federated‑learning collaboration, together with concrete compliance and KPI thresholds. The medium‑term segment ($3$–$5$ years) expands these pilots by introducing generative‑AI decision‑support, broader federated‑learning consortia, and real‑time digital‑twin prototypes, while formalising interoperable data standards and multi‑site governance frameworks. The long‑term vision ($5+$ years) envisions an autonomous, digital‑twin‑driven ecosystem that continuously integrates high‑frequency data, performs multi‑objective optimisation, and operates under a comprehensive ethical and regulatory governance structure. The following subsections detail each phase and the required capabilities, standards, metrics, and governance mechanisms.

## 7.1. Short‑Term (1‑2 years)

The short‑term (1–2 years) phase of the roadmap concentrates on AI interventions that can be built, validated, and deployed with modest technical effort while delivering measurable operational gains. These “low‑hanging‑fruit” projects focus on (i) predictive scheduling of admissions, staff rosters, and operating‑room blocks, (ii) lightweight resource‑allocation dashboards that visualise real‑time bed and equipment utilisation, and (iii) a pilot federated‑learning (FL) programme that enables multi‑site model improvement without centralising patient‑level data.

Predictive scheduling leverages the well‑defined combinatorial structures identified in the hospital‑scheduling literature, such as the patient‑admission scheduling problem, nurse‑rostering, and operating‑room allocation [33]. Metaheuristic search methods (e.g., tabu search, large‑neighbourhood search) and hybrid matheuristics can generate near‑optimal schedules within minutes, making them suitable for pilot deployment [33]. Complementary machine‑learning forecasts of admission volume, length of stay, and staffing demand—derived from historical EHR streams—provide the demand inputs required by these optimisation engines [87]. Reported gains from analogous implementations include reductions in patient waiting time and overtime, as well as higher bed‑occupancy rates [87].

Resource‑allocation dashboards translate the output of the scheduling engine into actionable visualisations for hospital managers. Real‑time integration with EHR or ADT feeds (typically via HL7 FHIR) supplies up‑to‑date occupancy, transfer frequency, and equipment‑usage metrics that can be displayed on wall‑mounted or web‑based panels [47][99]. Early pilots have demonstrated a 12 % decrease in average inpatient length of stay and a 25 % uplift in patient‑satisfaction scores after implementing such dashboards [47]. The dashboards also expose soft‑constraint penalty components (e.g., transfer penalty = 100, gender‑policy penalty ≈ 50) that can be monitored to ensure alignment with the optimisation objective [33].

A federated‑learning pilot is introduced to address the evidence gap identified in the literature regarding multi‑site model training for scheduling and resource‑allocation tasks [87][75]. The pilot will involve two to three partner hospitals that each retain their raw EHR data locally while exchanging encrypted model updates through a secure aggregation server. Differential‑privacy noise will be added to the updates to bound re‑identification risk, and a blockchain‑based audit log will record the provenance of each contribution [25][80]. The FL framework will be exercised on the publicly described synthetic datasets (13‑instance PASP and the 9‑family generator) to ensure reproducibility and to benchmark performance against centrally trained baselines [33].

**Compliance checklist (bullet points)**  
- **Hard‑constraint enforcement:** All capacity, gender, age, and quarantine rules must be satisfied exactly as defined in the scheduling formulation [33].  
- **Privacy safeguards:** Encrypt data at rest and in transit; apply differential‑privacy noise to FL updates and use secure aggregation for multi‑site training [25][80].  
- **Bias audit:** Conduct a bias assessment (e.g., demographic parity or equalized odds) on scheduling outcomes before deployment [25].  
- **Explainability:** Generate local explanations (e.g., SHAP values) for each schedule recommendation and archive them for audit purposes [25].  

**KPI targets for short‑term pilots**

| KPI | Target | Source |
|-----|--------|--------|
| Average patient‑waiting‑time reduction | ≥ 15 % relative decrease vs. baseline | [87][47] |
| Bed‑turnover improvement | ≥ 10 % increase in turnover rate vs. baseline | [87][47] |
| Economic impact (ROI) | Preliminary ROI ≥ 1.2 × pilot investment | [47] |

**Implementation workflow**

1. **Site selection** – choose one high‑throughput department (e.g., oncology outpatient scheduling) and one acute‑care unit (e.g., ED bed management) as pilot domains.  
2. **Baseline measurement** – collect 3 months of historical KPI data (waiting time, bed occupancy, overtime hours) to establish reference values.  
3. **Model development** – train a metaheuristic scheduler on the synthetic PASP data, calibrate soft‑constraint weights, and fine‑tune a demand‑forecasting ML model on local EHR extracts.  
4. **Dashboard construction** – integrate the scheduler output with a FHIR‑based data‑integration layer and visualise key metrics (occupancy, transfer frequency, penalty scores) on a web dashboard.  
5. **Federated‑learning trial** – connect the second pilot site via a secure FL orchestrator, exchange encrypted model updates for 20 communication rounds, and compare federated versus local model performance on the defined KPIs.  
6. **Compliance audit** – run the bias‑audit, XAI, and governance checklists; document results in the central compliance repository.  
7. **Pilot execution** – deploy the AI‑generated schedules for a 3‑month period, with clinician approval at each scheduling cycle; monitor KPI trajectories in real time.  
8. **Post‑pilot review** – analyse KPI improvements, audit findings, and cost‑benefit calculations; decide on scaling based on whether all evaluation thresholds are met.

By adhering to the above compliance and evaluation framework, the short‑term roadmap establishes a reproducible, evidence‑based foundation for AI‑enabled hospital management that can be reliably expanded in later phases.  

Having outlined the short‑term actions, the medium‑term roadmap will focus on scaling these pilots, extending federated‑learning collaborations, and introducing more advanced capabilities such as real‑time digital twins and generative‑AI decision support.

## 7.2. Medium‑Term (3‑5 years)

The medium‑term (3–5 years) phase of the integrated roadmap builds on the short‑term pilots by scaling generative‑AI‑enhanced decision‑support services, extending cross‑hospital federated‑learning collaborations, and deploying real‑time digital‑twin prototypes. At the same time, it formalises interoperable data‑exchange standards and comprehensive governance policies that together enable trustworthy, multi‑site AI ecosystems for hospital management.

Generative‑AI decision‑support systems will move from proof‑of‑concept to operational modules that synthesize heterogeneous clinical and operational data, generate scenario‑based recommendations, and continuously refine their knowledge through self‑supervised learning. The literature describes generative‑AI as capable of “high‑speed processing, pattern recognition, and predictive analytics” that uncover hidden inefficiencies and support human experts in insight generation and ethical judgment [75]. In nursing practice, generative‑AI‑driven clinical decision‑support has already been shown to integrate EHR, laboratory, and monitoring streams to produce actionable insights at the point of care [49]. However, systematic reviews highlight a scarcity of multi‑site, quantitative KPI evidence for such systems, underscoring the need for rigorously designed pilots that measure outcomes such as scheduling latency, resource‑utilisation gains, and cost avoidance [88][49].

Cross‑hospital federated‑learning (FL) networks will be expanded from isolated pilots to regional consortia that jointly train predictive models for admission forecasting, readmission risk, and resource allocation while keeping raw patient data on‑site. A three‑hospital case study in Nigeria demonstrated that a FedAvg model achieved an AUROC of 0.82 (within 1 % of a centrally trained model) and reduced membership‑inference attack success from 22 % to 8 %, confirming both predictive parity and privacy preservation [29]. Despite these promising results, a systematic review of FL in healthcare found that only 5.2 % of studies report real‑world clinical applications, indicating a substantial evidence gap that must be addressed through larger, longitudinal deployments [45]. Open‑source toolchains such as TenSEAL, PySyft/PyGrid, and TensorFlow Federated provide the technical foundation for scalable FL pipelines, while governance frameworks that incorporate secure aggregation, differential privacy, and blockchain‑based immutable audit trails ensure regulatory compliance and institutional trust [102][32].

Real‑time digital‑twin prototypes will be instantiated as continuous, high‑fidelity replicas of hospital operations that ingest live data via standardized HL7 FHIR interfaces and feed predictive analytics back to scheduling and resource‑allocation engines. An open‑source FHIR‑based framework for biomedical signal acquisition demonstrated sub‑second end‑to‑end latency, zero packet loss, and seamless integration of IoMT streams into analytics stacks, thereby establishing a viable data backbone for digital‑twin simulations [57]. The digital‑twin concept, defined as a “virtual replica … continuously synchronized with sensor data,” enables hospitals to run what‑if scenarios, anticipate bottlenecks, and evaluate policy interventions in silico before enactment [83][32]. Pilot deployments should target KPI clusters such as ≥ 10 % reduction in average length of stay, ≥ 15 % increase in bed‑turnover rate, and demonstrable cost‑effectiveness, with outcomes tracked through automated dashboards linked to the twin’s simulation engine.

Interoperable data standards constitute the connective tissue of these initiatives. HL7 FHIR is identified as the most widely implemented standard for modular integration and native support of third‑party AI applications [88]; it also serves as the canonical format for real‑time observations and command‑and‑control messages in digital‑twin pipelines [57]. Complementary use of the OMOP Common Data Model, enabled by bidirectional FHIR↔OMOP transformation tools, provides a structured, analytics‑ready layer for secondary use and federated model training [61]. Adoption of these standards across participating hospitals will reduce semantic mismatches, streamline API development, and facilitate the exchange of model updates and simulation parameters without bespoke ETL pipelines [88][57].

Governance policies will be codified in a multi‑site charter that aligns with the WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health and the U.S. ONC Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA), both of which advocate open standards, structured data sharing, and stakeholder co‑design [88]. A detailed compliance checklist—derived from privacy‑preserving FL frameworks and digital‑twin security guidelines—will mandate TLS encryption, role‑based access controls, differential‑privacy budgets, and blockchain‑anchored audit logs for every model‑update transaction [102][32][75]. Bias‑mitigation procedures will require demographic parity monitoring, fairness‑aware loss functions, and periodic re‑training on inclusive datasets, as recommended for equitable AI deployment [88][75]. Governance bodies comprising clinicians, data scientists, ethicists, and legal representatives will oversee continuous monitoring, explainability reporting (e.g., SHAP or counterfactual explanations), and post‑deployment impact assessments.

An evaluation framework will be instituted to capture both operational and economic outcomes. Core KPI targets include (i) ≥ 10 % reduction in average length of stay, (ii) ≥ 15 % improvement in bed‑turnover or equipment utilisation, (iii) ≥ 5 % cost‑savings in staffing or consumables, and (iv) ≤ 2 % disparity in performance metrics across protected patient groups. Economic analyses will employ cost‑effectiveness and budget‑impact models to translate efficiency gains into monetary terms, addressing the current paucity of cost data noted in recent horizon scans [87][49]. Continuous performance monitoring, bias audits, and explainability dashboards will feed back into the governance loop, ensuring that AI systems remain safe, transparent, and aligned with clinical objectives.

Having outlined the medium‑term trajectory, the subsequent section will consider the long‑term vision for AI‑driven hospital management beyond five years.

## 7.3. Long‑Term (5+ years)

The long‑term vision for hospital management is a fully integrated, autonomous scheduling and resource‑allocation ecosystem in which a real‑time digital twin (DT) serves as the operational nervous system. Continuous streams from bedside monitors, electronic health records, IoT sensors, and enterprise resource‑planning systems feed a high‑fidelity virtual replica of the entire care pathway; AI optimisation engines then generate instantaneous scheduling, staffing, and equipment‑allocation decisions that are enacted by the hospital information system and immediately reflected back into the twin for ongoing validation (Figure 1). This closed‑loop architecture enables the institution to anticipate demand spikes, re‑configure capacity on the fly, and maintain alignment with clinical priorities without human latency [55][23][15][20].

**Technical architecture**  
The autonomous loop can be decomposed into four interoperable layers.  
1. **Data‑integration layer** – bidirectional FHIR/HL7/OMOP pipelines ingest high‑frequency vital signs, admission/discharge events, and resource‑usage logs, applying privacy‑preserving transformations (e.g., differential privacy) before they reach the twin [55][15].  
2. **Digital‑twin simulation layer** – physics‑informed discrete‑event or agent‑based models continuously mirror patient flow, bed occupancy, and staff workload, calibrated in real time by the incoming data streams [23][20].  
3. **AI decision‑engine layer** – multi‑objective optimisation (e.g., ε‑constraint, reinforcement‑learning policies) consumes the twin’s state to produce prescriptive actions such as shift re‑allocation, OR block adjustments, or dynamic bed reassignment [15][20].  
4. **Execution & feedback layer** – recommendations are dispatched to scheduling and resource‑management modules; the resulting operational outcomes (e.g., actual wait times, overtime hours) are fed back to the twin, closing the monitoring loop and triggering model retraining where performance deviates from predefined thresholds [15][20].

**Governance and ethical scaffolding**  
A robust governance framework must be embedded across the entire lifecycle. The four‑phase ethical mapping proposed for digital‑twin development (design, data collection, analysis/simulation, deployment) provides a systematic checklist for privacy, autonomy, and beneficence considerations [56]. This can be combined with the SHIFT initiative, which operationalises six ethical characteristics—fairness, transparency, trustworthiness, accountability, privacy, and empathy—into concrete governance artefacts such as consent‑management modules, audit‑trail specifications, and stakeholder oversight panels [110]. The ethical dimensions identified in the broader digital‑twin literature (fairness & justice, autonomy, privacy, responsibility, transparency) further reinforce the need for equity‑focused data sampling, explainable AI interfaces, and clear liability matrices [84]. Finally, a flow‑oriented perspective recommends treating “transitional moments” (e.g., major schedule revisions, capacity expansions) as governance checkpoints where blockchain‑based immutable logs record data provenance and decision rationales, thereby ensuring traceability and regulatory compliance [91].

| Component | Governance action | Source |
|-----------|------------------|--------|
| Data‑collection | Privacy‑by‑design, consent verification, DP/noise addition | [56] |
| Model‑development | Value‑sensitive design, fairness audits, explainability (SHAP, counterfactuals) | [84] |
| Deployment | Blockchain audit trails, role‑based access control, continuous compliance reporting | [91] |
| Monitoring | Real‑time KPI dashboards, trigger thresholds for model drift, periodic ethical reviews | [15] |

**Continuous outcome monitoring**  
Operational success will be measured through a unified KPI suite that captures both efficiency and equity. Core metrics include average scheduling latency (target ≤ 5 min), bed‑turnover rate (≥ 15 % improvement), overtime hours (≥ 10 % reduction), patient‑wait‑time variance across demographic groups (≤ 2 % disparity), and incidence of privacy breaches (zero tolerance). The digital twin continuously computes these indicators and visualises them on interactive dashboards, enabling managers to detect performance degradations instantly and to initiate corrective re‑optimisation cycles [15][20][17]. The COF study demonstrated that integrating such KPI feeds into a twin can yield up to 90 % cost savings and a 96 % reduction in queue size, illustrating the magnitude of impact achievable when outcome monitoring is tightly coupled to the optimisation loop [17].

**Multi‑site, longitudinal evidence generation**  
Scaling the autonomous DT ecosystem requires federated‑learning (FL) networks that allow hospitals to co‑train predictive components (e.g., admission‑volume forecasts, no‑show probabilities) without exposing raw patient data. Evidence from a three‑hospital FL pilot shows that model utility remains within 1 % of a centrally trained baseline while membership‑inference attack success drops from 22 % to 8 %, confirming both predictive parity and privacy preservation [110][15]. To build a robust evidence base, longitudinal studies spanning ≥ 5 years should record KPI trajectories, bias metrics, and economic outcomes (cost‑avoidance, ROI) across participating sites, thereby addressing the current paucity of KPI‑rich, multi‑site reports highlighted in recent scoping reviews [106][20].

**Research gaps and roadmap actions**  
Despite promising prototypes, several barriers must be resolved before the vision can be realised:  

* **Standardisation** – Uniform adoption of HL7 FHIR, OMOP, and ISO 42001 AI‑management standards is required to eliminate semantic friction between heterogeneous hospital IT stacks [91][20].  
* **Validation frameworks** – Rigorous statistical validation (prediction‑error confidence intervals, hypothesis testing) and health‑economic evaluation (budget‑impact, cost‑effectiveness) remain scarce and should be embedded in pilot protocols [110][106].  
* **Scalability of simulation** – High‑performance computing resources (GPU clusters, edge‑computing nodes) must be orchestrated to sustain sub‑second twin updates for large, multi‑site networks [55][20].  
* **Regulatory alignment** – Early engagement with FDA SaMD pathways, EU AI Act classifications, and GDPR/HIPAA compliance checks will streamline certification of autonomous scheduling modules [110][56].  
* **Human‑in‑the‑loop safeguards** – Governance panels should mandate clinician sign‑off for any schedule change that exceeds a predefined risk threshold, preserving professional autonomy while leveraging AI efficiency [84][10].

Addressing these gaps will involve coordinated actions: (i) develop open‑source FHIR‑based data‑exchange kits for DT ingestion; (ii) create a consortium‑wide governance charter that codifies audit‑trail, bias‑monitoring, and liability responsibilities; (iii) launch multi‑institutional FL pilots focused on scheduling and resource‑allocation KPIs; and (iv) publish longitudinal KPI dashboards to enable continuous learning and policy refinement.

Having outlined the long‑term architecture, governance, and evidence‑generation strategy, the subsequent conclusion will synthesize these insights and propose concrete directions for future research and implementation.

# 8. Conclusion & Future Research Directions

The review demonstrates that artificial‑intelligence techniques have begun to permeate the three core operational pillars of hospital management—scheduling, resource allocation, and patient‑flow optimisation. Predictive analytics and reinforcement‑learning policies have been shown to improve appointment adherence, operating‑room makespan, and staff‑rostering efficiency [4][112][97][100], while optimisation engines for bed, ICU, and consumable management can increase occupancy rates and reduce waste [97][69][13]. Emerging AI modalities further extend these capabilities: large‑language models enable natural‑language interfaces and automated protocol generation [39][111][94]; federated learning permits collaborative model development across institutions without exposing raw patient data [8][29]; and real‑time digital twins provide a continuously synchronised virtual replica that supports descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics for capacity planning [31][74].

Across the literature, reported operational gains include reductions in wait times, improvements in scheduling accuracy, and higher resource utilisation. However, the evidence base is limited in several respects. Most studies are single‑site pilots with short observation windows, preventing assessment of longitudinal impact and generalisability [67][82]. Economic evaluations are scarce, leaving the cost‑benefit profile of AI‑enabled interventions largely unquantified [67]. Explainability and fairness analyses are often confined to post‑hoc visualisations rather than integrated, clinically actionable tools, and scalability to multi‑hospital ecosystems remains under‑explored [87]. Governance and regulatory considerations are frequently discussed in principle but lack concrete, empirically validated frameworks that link compliance activities to measurable outcomes [28][79].

To advance AI‑driven hospital management from promising prototypes to evidence‑based, scalable solutions, future research should prioritise the following avenues. First, conduct multi‑site, longitudinal studies that track key performance indicators (e.g., wait‑time, bed‑turnover, readmission rates) over extended periods and across diverse organisational contexts, thereby establishing external validity and informing transferability [67][82]. Second, develop and rigorously test explainable‑AI methods that provide transparent, real‑time rationales for scheduling and allocation decisions, with systematic fairness audits to detect and mitigate bias across demographic groups [28][79]. Third, design federated‑learning pipelines that combine differential‑privacy mechanisms, secure aggregation, and blockchain‑based audit trails, and evaluate their predictive parity, communication efficiency, and privacy guarantees in realistic hospital networks [8][29][70]. Fourth, validate real‑time digital‑twin platforms through controlled experiments that quantify their impact on operational KPIs, model‑drift detection, and decision latency, while assessing the computational resources required for sub‑second updates at scale [31][74][83]. Fifth, embed comprehensive health‑economic analyses—including cost‑effectiveness, budget‑impact, and return‑on‑investment modelling—into pilot evaluations to provide decision‑makers with actionable financial evidence [67][87]. Sixth, advance interoperable data‑exchange standards (FHIR, OMOP, ISO 42001) and develop open‑source integration toolkits that reduce semantic friction and enable seamless coupling of AI components with existing hospital information systems [88][57]. Finally, construct multidisciplinary governance structures that operationalise ethical principles (autonomy, fairness, accountability, transparency) into enforceable policies, continuous monitoring dashboards, and regulatory‑compliant documentation, ensuring that AI deployments remain trustworthy throughout their lifecycle [76][79].

In relation to the three research questions posed in the introduction—(1) how AI methods currently support each operational pillar and what impact they have on performance indicators; (2) how generative AI, federated learning, and digital twins can be integrated to enhance effectiveness and scalability; and (3) what evidence gaps remain that must be addressed to build an integrated, evidence‑based roadmap—the review provides partial answers. The evidence confirms that AI improves scheduling accuracy, resource utilisation, and patient‑flow metrics, and it outlines concrete integration pathways for emerging technologies. Nonetheless, gaps in multi‑site validation, economic evaluation, and governance persist. The short‑term roadmap therefore focuses on low‑effort pilots with explicit compliance checklists and KPI thresholds [87]; the medium‑term roadmap expands these pilots, introduces generative‑AI decision‑support, broadens federated‑learning consortia, and deploys real‑time digital‑twin prototypes [88][32]; and the long‑term vision envisions an autonomous, digital‑twin‑driven ecosystem supported by robust standards, longitudinal evidence generation, and multidisciplinary governance [91][20].
