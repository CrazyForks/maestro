# 2. Theoretical Framework and Literature Review

The academic investigation into the psychology of decision-making within management has undergone a significant paradigm shift, moving beyond the traditional assumption of rational actors to embrace a pluralistic view that integrates behavioral insights. This evolution is characterized by a historical progression from foundational economic theories to modern "micro-foundations" that focus on the behavioral aspects of sensing and seizing opportunities. The literature traces this development from the foundational work of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1975) to the "micro-foundations" movement (Felin, Foss, & Ployhart, 2015), which emphasizes the cognitive processes underlying strategic decision-making. A critical theoretical debate exists between the "heuristics-and-biases" approach, which views biases as cognitive defects, and Gerd Gigerenzer's "fast and frugal" heuristics, which argues for their adaptive utility in natural settings and management contexts [14][50].

The integration of behavioral insights has been pivotal in expanding the scope of established management theories. Transaction Cost Theory (TCT), for instance, has been integrated with behavioral insights to explain governance choices, specifically how managers perceive and incorporate "behavioral uncertainty" into their analyses [50]. Furthermore, Agency Theory has been extended to incorporate insights from behavioral theories to relax traditional assumptions regarding self-interest and bounded rationality, providing a more nuanced understanding of the principal-agent relationship [69][47]. This theoretical expansion extends to the Resource-Based View (RBV) and Dynamic Capabilities framework, where the focus shifts to how psychological traits and heuristics influence the sensing and seizing of opportunities [41][50].

The literature distinguishes between strategic management decisions—such as international business and horizontal integration—and operational decisions, such as monitoring employees and HR practices [50]. In the context of strategic decisions, the "heuristics-and-biases" approach, exemplified by Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), is applied to explain cognitive biases in decision-making under risk [41]. Conversely, operational decisions are often analyzed through the lens of procedural rationality and bounded rationality, which highlight the limitations in human information-processing speed and flexibility [16][39]. The literature notes a significant gap in ecological validity regarding vignette studies, suggesting that while theoretical frameworks are robust, empirical validation across diverse operational contexts remains a challenge [57][73].

Theoretical frameworks also emphasize the distinction between behavioral integration in diverse management domains. Studies have shown that established management theories, such as Hambrick and Mason (1984), have been integrated with behavioral insights to posit that managerial traits act as catalysts for strategic firm actions [41]. This integration is crucial for understanding how cognitive biases manifest in various sectors, ranging from healthcare and education to government and non-profit organizations [62]. By synthesizing these theories, the literature provides a comprehensive foundation for understanding how cognitive biases influence decision-making, moving from macro-level strategic orientations to micro-level cognitive processes and individual differences.

# 3. Prominent Cognitive Biases in the Workplace

Cognitive biases represent a significant threat to effective decision-making within professional environments, manifesting in various distortions of judgment and perception. The following analysis initiates with Confirmation Bias, a fundamental cognitive distortion where individuals selectively seek and interpret information to confirm pre-existing beliefs, a phenomenon that exacerbates group polarization and strategic inertia. This is followed by a detailed examination of Anchoring Bias, which describes the pervasive tendency to rely too heavily on the initial piece of information encountered, a distortion that critically impacts negotiation dynamics, forecasting accuracy, and resource allocation. Finally, the section explores Overconfidence Bias, where individuals overestimate their predictive accuracy and risk tolerance, a cognitive error that frequently leads to miscalibration in project management and financial contexts. ## 3.1. Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias represents a fundamental cognitive distortion where individuals selectively seek, interpret, and recall information that confirms pre-existing hypotheses or beliefs while disregarding contradictory evidence [64][6]. Mechanisms of this bias include "motivated reasoning," where information is processed to align with beliefs, and the "positive test strategy," where individuals phrase questions to elicit affirmations that support their initial stance rather than testing for falsification [64][46].

The impact of confirmation bias on decision quality is significant; it leads to suboptimal judgments, overestimation of belief validity, and the underestimation of alternative viewpoints, ultimately compromising strategic outcomes [64][27]. In financial contexts, the bias is identified as a "high impact" factor that hurts the decision-making process and influences investment behaviors despite the support of decision systems [45][27].

In group dynamics, confirmation bias exacerbates phenomena such as groupthink and the formation of echo chambers. It fosters polarization, where views become more extreme, and can trigger status conflict by suppressing dissenting opinions necessary for effective collaboration [64][49]. In the workplace, this bias manifests as favoritism in talent acquisition and the reinforcement of flawed corporate strategies, leading to strategic inertia and missed opportunities [59][11].

The financial repercussions of unmitigated confirmation bias are substantial; studies indicate that decision-making failures due to cognitive biases can cost businesses up to 15% of revenue [48]. Conversely, implementing structured mitigation strategies, such as scenario planning and data-driven frameworks, can significantly improve decision quality and reduce these financial risks [48][38].

Having examined the mechanisms of information seeking that confirms pre-existing beliefs, the following section will analyze Anchoring Bias, which describes the cognitive tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information encountered.

## 3.2. Anchoring Bias

Anchoring bias is defined as the cognitive tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information encountered (the "anchor") when making decisions, often leading to insufficient adjustments and skewed final judgments [61][9]. Rooted in Tversky and Kahneman's "anchoring and adjustment" heuristic, the bias arises from the "selective accessibility model," where individuals compare the anchor to the target, retrieve selective knowledge consistent with the anchor, and rely on this accessible information [61]. This phenomenon is pervasive across diverse judgment scenarios, including legal sentencing and forecasting, and persists even when anchors are incorrect or implausible [54].

In professional contexts, particularly in talent acquisition and salary negotiations, anchoring serves as a critical bargaining tactic. Research indicates that whoever initiates the first offer often gains a strategic advantage, influencing the final negotiation outcome [61][12]. In salary negotiations, for instance, the initial offer (the anchor) significantly impacts the agreement; a low initial salary anchor by an employer can lead employees to accept lower compensation than they would otherwise [9]. This dynamic extends to performance evaluations, where initial assumptions can anchor subsequent judgments regarding employee potential [7].

Beyond interpersonal interactions, anchoring significantly impacts strategic decision-making and resource allocation. In strategic management, such as equity crowdfunding or corporate budgeting, the first signal or initial benchmark often dictates subsequent planning, potentially leading to "misaligned strategies" and an inability to recalibrate based on new data [43][17]. Similarly, in forecasting contexts, service level targets act as implicit anchors; high targets lead to overforecasting while low targets lead to underforecasting, reducing overall forecast accuracy [13]. This cognitive distortion is also observed in financial markets, where investors often anchor to initial purchase prices or historical data, affecting investment choices and asset allocation [8][40].

Susceptibility to anchoring varies based on individual characteristics, including mood, personality, and expertise. Sadness increases susceptibility compared to happiness, while personality traits such as high conscientiousness and agreeableness are linked to greater bias, whereas a desire for challenge reduces it [61][9]. While domain-specific expertise generally reduces the effect, even experts remain susceptible, as the "consider-the-opposite" strategy remains a vital mitigation technique [3][12]. To mitigate the influence of anchors, decision-makers are encouraged to employ strategies such as "considering the opposite," which involves generating logical reasons why an anchor is inappropriate [61][7]. Additionally, establishing a BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) and acquiring in-depth subject knowledge can help eliminate the undue influence of the initial value [61][3].

Having analyzed the mechanisms of anchoring and its influence on negotiation and strategy, the following section will examine Overconfidence Bias, a cognitive distortion where individuals overestimate their knowledge and predictive accuracy.

## 3.3. Overconfidence Bias

Overconfidence bias is defined as the cognitive distortion where individuals overestimate their own abilities, knowledge, and the accuracy of their predictions, often simultaneously underestimating risks [52]. This bias manifests through two primary dimensions: the "better-than-average" effect (overplacement), where individuals view themselves as superior to peers, and "overprecision," the tendency to claim greater certainty about an estimate than is objectively warranted [60]. In the context of management and professional forecasting, overconfidence is a pervasive issue that leads to miscalibration, where confidence intervals are set too narrowly and risk assessments are systematically underestimated [1][77].

The impact of overconfidence on decision-making is particularly acute in project management and financial forecasting. In software development and project planning, professionals frequently underestimate the uncertainty of effort estimates, leading to poor planning and execution failures [66]. This bias causes managers to focus exclusively on how objectives will be accomplished successfully, ignoring negative scenarios and assuming risks will not occur [31]. Empirical evidence suggests that experts are just as susceptible to this bias as laypeople; in fact, the most in-demand experts are often the most overconfident, leading to the underestimation of necessary risk mitigation measures [77][56]. This phenomenon extends to leadership dynamics, where individuals with a strong "sense of power" exhibit significantly higher levels of overprecision and lower accuracy in their judgments, as seen in high-stakes failures such as the BP oil spill and the AOL/Time Warner merger [32].

In financial contexts, overconfidence drives behaviors such as overtrading, the disposition effect (holding onto losing assets due to overestimation of skill), and the pursuit of aggressive strategies in mergers and acquisitions [10][32]. It creates a disconnect between perceived competence and actual performance, often resulting in corporate crises and poor investment outcomes [52]. To quantify this bias, researchers utilize metrics such as the Overestimation Score ($b - a$) and calibration bias ($|b - a|$) to measure the gap between perceived confidence and actual performance [1]. Furthermore, studies distinguish between short-term and long-term forecasting to analyze temporal differences in miscalibration [60].

*   **Mechanisms and Cognitive Drivers:** The bias is driven by heuristics such as the "priority heuristic," which focuses on outcomes before considering probabilities, and the "availability heuristic," where low availability of risk incidents in the mind leads to lower perceived risk probabilities [31]. The "WYSIATI" (What You See Is All There Is) principle also contributes, where individuals construct coherent patterns from available information while suppressing doubt and ambiguity [21].
*   **Measurement Approaches:** Quantification relies on comparing self-assessed confidence against actual accuracy, often using the Brier Score or decile-based probability distributions to distinguish between skill and miscalibration [58][60].
*   **Mitigation Strategies:** Evidence-based interventions include utilizing "estimation error information from previous projects" to ground uncertainty assessments in reality [66]. Organizations can mitigate bias by implementing "second independent forecasts" for high-stakes decisions, which regress the first forecast toward the mean, and by separating the product selection decision from the forecasting decision to eliminate selection-driven bias [70][55]. Additionally, structured feedback and the utilization of historical data have shown promise in reducing the "overprecision" of managers [66][4].

# 4. Financial Decision-Making Biases

Financial decision-making is profoundly shaped by cognitive biases that systematically distort forecasting accuracy and capital allocation strategies. The analysis begins with budgeting and forecasting errors, examining how optimism and recency biases lead to systematic errors in resource allocation and financial planning. This discussion extends to investment decisions, where loss aversion and the sunk cost fallacy drive managers toward risky, non-economic choices in areas such as technology projects and research and development. This section investigates the financial implications of these behaviors, highlighting how psychological factors override economic rationality in critical domains.

## 4.1. Budgeting and Forecasting Errors

Financial forecasting and resource allocation are critical processes where cognitive biases, particularly optimism and recency biases, significantly distort accuracy. These biases manifest in how financial managers perceive risk and predict future performance, leading to systematic errors in budgeting and capital planning.

Optimism bias is a pervasive distortion in financial decision-making, characterized by the systematic overestimation of positive outcomes and the underestimation of risks. In the context of budgeting, this bias is often driven by the "planning fallacy," where decision-makers focus on future goals rather than historical data, leading to overly optimistic predictions regarding costs and timelines [74][65]. This manifests as "narrow confidence intervals" in forecasts, where managers underestimate uncertainty and overestimate their ability to control outcomes [60]. The impact of this bias is profound; optimistic managers often perceive external financing as too costly, rejecting positive NPV projects (underinvestment) or accepting negative NPV projects when free cash flow is available (overinvestment) [2]. Empirical evidence suggests that this upward bias is not limited to private firms; official government budget agencies generate forecasts that are systematically positively biased, contributing to fiscal deficits [63].

Quantitatively, the effects of optimism bias are measurable through "optimism bias uplifts," which represent the percentage increase in projected costs relative to the baseline [74]. Studies analyzing transport projects and government budgets indicate that these uplifts can be substantial, with traditional procurement showing significant overestimation in capital expenditures [65][63]. Furthermore, the "hard-easy effect" suggests that managers are particularly prone to overconfidence when forecasting challenging financial scenarios, exacerbating the error [60].

In contrast to the long-term focus of optimism bias, recency bias drives decision-making based on the most recent information rather than historical trends. This bias is particularly relevant in forecasting, where the most recent yearly return disproportionately influences future expectations, often overpowering complex historical data [26]. The influence of recency is also evident in operational resource allocation, where decision-makers engage in "demand chasing." This behavior involves setting pricing thresholds and resource allocation based on the most recently observed willingness to pay, rather than objective profitability models [67]. Consequently, this leads to suboptimal revenue patterns and "inventory mis-sensitivity," where left-over capacity is misallocated [67].

The consequences of these biases extend to market behavior and asset pricing. Recency bias can cause investors to exhibit "panic selling" during downturns or "bubble buying" during rallies, driven by the immediate impact of recent events [45]. Research utilizing the Chronological Return Ordering (CRO) variable has identified a distinct asset pricing anomaly where the recent performance of an asset distorts its valuation, leading to abnormal returns that persist across various market conditions [75]. Moreover, the "hot hand" fallacy in fund management encourages investors to chase recent outperformers, further skewing resource allocation decisions away from long-term strategic goals [25].

Having examined how optimism and recency biases distort financial projections and resource allocation, the following section will analyze the specific risks associated with investment and capital allocation.

## 4.2. Investment and Capital Allocation

The impact of cognitive biases on corporate investment strategies is profound, particularly through the lens of the sunk cost fallacy and loss aversion. These behavioral distortions lead to suboptimal capital allocation, where financial resources are misdirected toward failing ventures or retained in underperforming assets due to psychological attachment rather than economic viability.

**Loss Aversion and Risk Appetite**

The foundational driver of these investment biases is loss aversion, a cognitive bias rooted in Prospect Theory. This theory posits that losses are weighted approximately twice as heavily as equivalent gains, creating a psychological "long grief of losses" rather than short-term celebration of positive outcomes [76]. For corporate decision-makers, this manifests as a risk-averse posture regarding potential losses, often leading to the refusal to cut losses on underperforming assets—a behavior known as the disposition effect [34]. Empirical evidence indicates that this bias negatively impacts corporate performance; studies have found a negative correlation between loss aversion and Return on Assets (ROA) across various sectors, suggesting that managers prioritize avoiding losses over pursuing optimal investment returns [44]. Consequently, this bias influences risk appetite, where managers exhibit a reluctance to realize losses, leading to capital being tied up in underperforming investments [36].

**The Sunk Cost Fallacy and Escalation of Commitment**

The sunk cost fallacy, or escalation of commitment, is characterized by the irrational continuation of a behavior due to previously committed resources rather than future utility. This phenomenon, often termed the "Concorde Fallacy" (after the supersonic airliner program), explains why decision-makers continue to fund ventures despite clear evidence of unviability [76]. Research identifies personal responsibility as a primary driver, where managers "double down" to protect ego and validate initial choices, regardless of negative outcomes [76]. This bias is not merely theoretical; it manifests as a strong tendency to delay project termination and a resistance to asset disposal at market value, as individuals set prices above market to justify sunk expenditures [24].

**Impact on Capital Allocation and Strategic Decisions**

The financial implications of these biases are severe, distorting capital allocation across critical domains:

*   **IT and Technology Projects:** The sunk cost fallacy leads to significant financial waste; studies indicate that 66% of technology projects end in partial or total failure, resulting in billions in wasted resources [76]. Corporate examples such as the FBI Virtual Case File and the Denver International Airport baggage system illustrate how massive overruns occur due to the inability to abandon failed initiatives.
*   **Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A):** In M&A, the "due diligence investment trap" creates layers of sunk costs (legal fees, advisory costs) that make walking away psychologically difficult. Research indicates that cost shocks during deals reduce divestiture rates, as seen in cases like the Bayer-Monsanto acquisition, where continued investment occurred despite mounting concerns [68]. Empirical analysis of fixed exchange ratio stock mergers shows that exogenous market fluctuations predict subsequent divestiture rates, revealing how sunk costs distort exit strategies [68].
*   **Research and Development (R&D):** The bias manifests in the persistence of low-probability ventures. Approximately 90% of clinical drug development efforts fail, with managers continuing to invest despite the statistical improbability of success [76]. In R&D contexts, such as the Omega hybrid engine project, managers may continue investment (despite competitor superiority) simply because 5 million PLN has already been invested, illustrating the "spending financial resources in their maintenance" to recover losses [36][24].

**Neurobiological Mechanisms and Counter-Intuitive Effects**

Neuroscience provides insight into why these biases persist despite economic rationality. Sunk cost decisions activate the insula (associated with negative emotional processing) and reduce activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (responsible for rational value computation), shifting decision-making from rational evaluation to emotional processing [76]. Experimental analysis suggests a nuance in this behavior: a "robust reverse sunk cost effect" is observed where the size of the initial investment inversely influences continuation decisions, suggesting that in some contexts, large losses trigger a desire to avoid further loss rather than a compulsion to recover the original stake [28].

## 5.1. Talent Acquisition and Performance Reviews

Cognitive biases manifest acutely in human resource management, critically influencing talent acquisition and performance appraisal processes. The pervasive influence of the Halo Effect and Affinity Bias distorts objective decision-making, often leading to the selection of suboptimal candidates or the inaccurate evaluation of employee performance.

The Halo Effect describes a cognitive distortion where a positive impression in one area—such as physical attractiveness, mannerisms, or initial demeanor—skews the evaluator's perception of competence or overall quality in other areas [30]. In recruitment contexts, recruiters often disproportionately weight these superficial signals, leading to the assumption that attractive or charismatic candidates possess superior skills and intelligence, regardless of objective merit [71][42]. This bias is frequently driven by stereotypes across various cultural backgrounds and genders, resulting in evaluations based on visual cues rather than hard skills [71][18]. The consequences are severe; relying on this bias can lead to the "brain drain" of qualified talent in favor of individuals who fit a superficial ideal, compromising organizational performance [71][33]. Mitigation strategies focus on removing visual cues; techniques such as blind auditions and anonymized resumes (adopted by organizations like HSBC and KPMG) have proven effective in shifting focus from appearance to merit [42][30].

Affinity Bias, closely related to the Halo Effect, involves an unconscious preference for individuals who share similar backgrounds, experiences, or social identities—a manifestation of Social Identity Theory [29]. This bias leads decision-makers to favor candidates from their own alma maters, those who fit a specific cultural mold, or individuals from similar demographic groups, often overlooking superior qualifications [35][53]. In employee evaluations, this manifests as "in-group favoritism," where managers rate or promote those they perceive as socially connected or similar to themselves, favoring "comfort" over merit [35][72]. The resulting "mirrortocracy" (a meritocracy based on personal connection) stifles diversity, limits innovation through homogenous teams, and creates legal risks for organizations [29][53]. Mitigation requires structural changes, such as blinding hiring processes to remove demographic identifiers and implementing diverse hiring panels to disrupt this unconscious preference [35][22].

Finally, biases also distort performance appraisals through phenomena such as the Halo and Horn Effects. These biases cause ratings to be excessively similar across different dimensions or unfairly negative based on initial impressions, leading to "leniency" errors where high performers are rated lower or low performers are rated higher depending on the rater's initial impression [15][72]. Implementing structured feedback mechanisms, such as 360-degree reviews, is critical to counteracting individual rater biases by incorporating multiple perspectives on performance [20].

## 5.2. Leadership and Team Dynamics

Cognitive biases play a pivotal role in leadership decision-making, often skewing judgment and strategic direction. Overconfidence is identified as the most recurrent bias affecting professionals, manifesting as the "better-than-average" effect and an overestimation of personal competence [59][73]. This bias is particularly acute in leaders and top executives, where power activates "self-serving cognition" and an "illusion of control," leading to miscalibrated risk assessments and suboptimal outcomes [37][60]. Furthermore, Optimism Bias and the Planning Fallacy are prevalent, causing leaders to overestimate success odds and underestimate failure risks, which can result in complacency and a lack of corrective action [78][23].

A significant risk in leadership is Groupthink, a cognitive bias arising from a yearning for cohesion within a group that leads to irrational or dysfunctional decision-making [59]. This phenomenon is exacerbated when decision-makers are under pressure and prioritize conformity over objective analysis [78]. The literature highlights structural causes for Groupthink, including leader power encouraging deference to superiors and insufficient diversity within the group, which stifles necessary dissent [5]. The presence of Confirmation Bias further fuels this dynamic, as leaders may seek out information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while ignoring contradictory evidence [51].

Beyond individual biases, cognitive biases significantly impact team dynamics and cohesion. Status conflict often arises when goal misalignment triggers personal agendas rather than task-focused goals, negatively impacting team performance [19]. While shared leadership serves as a critical moderator that strengthens positive relationships and mitigates interpersonal tensions, the presence of biases like Affinity Bias can disrupt this cohesion by favoring in-group members based on social identity [79][51]. Additionally, Affective conflict—characterized by personal animosity—directly disrupts communication and trust, whereas the presence of Overconfidence can exert detrimental effects on procedural resources and social cohesion if unchecked [52][19].

To mitigate these risks, leadership must cultivate a culture of critical thinking and actively seek diverse information sources. Strategies such as assigning a "devil's advocate," utilizing structured decision-making frameworks like the Delphi Technique or Project Pre-mortems, and implementing "vigilant problem-solving" are essential to counteract the "paralysis by analysis" and ensure objective decision-making [78][5]. By challenging assumptions and fostering an environment where dissenting views are valued, leaders can reduce the influence of these cognitive distortions on team performance and organizational success.
